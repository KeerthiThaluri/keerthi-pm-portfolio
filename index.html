How I think through a product problem

I like to start with the person using the product, get clear on the problem, and then work out what would actually count as improvement.

01 · Discover

Define the user, context, pain point, and what evidence is still missing.

02 · Define

Frame a testable problem and clarify desired outcomes and non-goals.

03 · Prioritise

Compare ideas by impact, effort, feasibility, risks, and user trust.

04 · Measure

Define primary metrics, guardrails, event tracking, and an honest learning plan.

Transparency note: These are independent portfolio concepts, not products I have launched for employers. The hypotheses, metrics, and experiment plans are proposals, not validated findings. No user research, A/B test outcomes, business impact, or domain partnerships are claimed unless explicitly added later with evidence.

EdTech · Independent case study← All case studies

Helping learners build a consistent study habit

Domain: EdTech

Project type: Independent product case study

Status: Independent concept; hypotheses and proposed metrics are not validated with learners.

1. Problem framing

Learners may start a course with strong intent but struggle to return regularly when lessons feel long or progress is hard to see.

2. Target users

Self-paced learners balancing study with work, college, and family commitments.

3. Hypotheses to validate

  • If learners can choose a small daily learning goal, more will complete a first lesson and return the next day.
  • If reminders are based on a learner's chosen study window, lesson completion may improve without increasing notification opt-outs.

4. Proposed product direction

  • Offer a learner-selected daily goal (for example, 5, 10, or 15 minutes).
  • Show a clear next lesson and progress indicator on the home screen.
  • Let learners choose reminder timing and pause reminders easily.

5. Measurement plan

  • Primary candidate: first-week lesson completion rate.
  • Secondary: time to first lesson, lessons completed per active learner, D1/D7 retention.
  • Guardrails: reminder opt-out rate, course abandonment, accessibility issues.

6. Experiment approach

Test a progress-led home screen against the current experience. Predefine eligible users, a minimum detectable effect, sample size, duration, and guardrails before launch.

7. Risks and guardrails

Streak mechanics can create pressure or reward time spent over learning quality. Keep goals flexible and avoid punitive streak loss.

8. What remains to be done

  • Validate the problem with user research or trustworthy secondary research.
  • Sketch the current and proposed journey.
  • Define event properties and data-quality checks.
  • Confirm baseline metrics and experiment eligibility before proposing sample size.
  • Record findings honestly; do not invent results.

This is an independent product-thinking exercise. The problem framing, proposed solutions, and metrics are hypotheses; no user research or live experiments are claimed.

Reducing friction in appointment booking

Domain: HealthTech

Project type: Independent product case study

Status: Independent concept; no patient research or live experiment has been conducted.

1. Problem framing

Patients may abandon booking when availability, preparation instructions, or appointment details are unclear.

2. Target users

People trying to find an appropriate appointment while balancing urgency, location, timing, and cost.

3. Hypotheses to validate

  • Showing available slots earlier may reduce search effort.
  • A clear booking summary and preparation checklist may reduce uncertainty before confirmation.

4. Proposed product direction

  • Let users filter by date, location, specialty, and appointment type.
  • Show total cost and cancellation rules before confirmation when available.
  • Provide a concise booking summary and preparation checklist.

5. Measurement plan

  • Primary candidate: booking completion rate.
  • Secondary: time to book, search-to-slot-selection rate, reschedule completion.
  • Guardrails: booking errors, cancellations, accessibility, privacy and consent issues.

6. Experiment approach

Compare the existing booking flow with a version that exposes availability earlier. Segment results by device and appointment type only where sample sizes support interpretation.

7. Risks and guardrails

Health data is sensitive. Avoid unnecessary data collection, provide accessible flows, and never imply clinical triage or medical advice from a booking optimisation.

8. What remains to be done

  • Validate the problem with user research or trustworthy secondary research.
  • Sketch the current and proposed journey.
  • Define event properties and data-quality checks.
  • Confirm baseline metrics and experiment eligibility before proposing sample size.
  • Record findings honestly; do not invent results.

Domain: FinTech

Project type: Independent product case study

Status: Independent concept; no financial data or user outcomes are claimed.

1. Problem framing

Users may see transaction totals but still struggle to understand spending patterns or decide what action to take.

2. Target users

Early-career adults who want a simple overview of spending without complex financial jargon.

3. Hypotheses to validate

  • A category-level weekly summary may help users recognise spending patterns faster.
  • User-controlled budget alerts may be more useful than generic notifications.

4. Proposed product direction

  • Show category totals with plain-language explanations.
  • Allow users to set optional budgets and alert thresholds.
  • Make transaction categorisation editable and explain estimates or missing data.

5. Measurement plan

  • Primary candidate: weekly insight engagement followed by a meaningful action (e.g., review or edit a budget).
  • Secondary: categorisation correction rate, budget setup completion, repeat use.
  • Guardrails: incorrect categorisation, alert opt-outs, user trust complaints, privacy incidents.

6. Experiment approach

Test a concise weekly summary against a basic totals view. Do not equate clicks with financial wellbeing; assess whether users understand the information.

7. Risks and guardrails

Avoid prescriptive investment advice, misleading certainty, or shame-based language. Make data sources and categorisation limitations clear.

8. What remains to be done

Project type: Independent product case study

1. Problem framing

Returning customers may need to repeat the same search and customisation steps even when they already know what they want.

2. Target users

3. Hypotheses to validate

  • A reliable reorder entry point may reduce time from app open to checkout.
  • Remembered preferences may reduce repeated effort if they are visible and editable.

4. Proposed product direction

  • Add a recent-order shortcut with current availability and price checks.
  • Show saved customisations before adding an item to cart.

6. Experiment approach

Compare a recent-order shortcut with the current discovery path for eligible repeat users; ensure price and availability are refreshed.

7. Risks and guardrails

Saved dietary preferences must not be treated as guarantees against allergens. Always show current item details and allow edits.

8. What remains to be done

Reducing uncertainty between booking and pickup

Domain: Mobility

Project type: Independent product case study

Status: Independent concept; operational feasibility and user impact are unvalidated.

1. Problem framing

Riders and drivers may struggle to find each other when pickup instructions are vague or location pins do not reflect the real meeting point.

2. Target users

Riders and drivers meeting in crowded, multi-exit, or unfamiliar pickup locations.

3. Hypotheses to validate

  • More specific pickup guidance may reduce contact attempts and pickup delays.
  • A shared landmark description may improve rider-driver coordination.

4. Proposed product direction

  • Allow a short, structured pickup note with suggested landmarks.
  • Show the same pickup description to rider and driver.
  • Provide an easy way to report an inaccessible or unsafe pickup point.

5. Measurement plan

  • Primary candidate: median time from driver arrival to trip start.
  • Secondary: pickup-related calls/messages, pickup changes, cancellation rate.
  • Guardrails: safety reports, wrong-pickup incidents, accessibility, location privacy.

6. Experiment approach

Pilot in a limited set of pickup contexts and compare pickup outcomes while monitoring safety and cancellation guardrails.

7. Risks and guardrails

Location data is sensitive. Do not expose unnecessary precise location history; ensure safety reports are handled appropriately.

8. What remains to be done

  • Validate the problem with user research or trustworthy secondary research.
  • Sketch the current and proposed journey.
  • Define event properties and data-quality checks.

Helping shoppers compare products with confidence

Domain: E-commerce

Project type: Independent product case study

Status: Independent concept; proposed flow and metrics are not validated against commerce data.

1. Problem framing

Shoppers may switch between product pages to compare specifications, total price, delivery, and return conditions.

2. Target users

Considered-purchase shoppers comparing similar products before buying.

3. Hypotheses to validate

  • A side-by-side comparison view may reduce repeated navigation.
  • Surfacing total cost and return terms consistently may help shoppers make informed decisions.

4. Proposed product direction

  • Let shoppers compare a small number of products on key attributes.
  • Highlight differences rather than repeating identical specifications.
  • Show total price, delivery estimate, warranty, and return terms in a consistent place.

5. Measurement plan

  • Primary candidate: comparison-to-purchase conversion.
  • Secondary: comparison feature usage, time to decision, product detail revisits.
  • Guardrails: returns, cancellations, comparison errors, page performance.

6. Experiment approach

Test comparison availability on a selected category. Segment by product complexity and ensure attribute data is accurate before rollout.

7. Risks and guardrails

A comparison table can mislead if product attributes are missing or inconsistent. Show unknown values explicitly and avoid biased ordering.

8. What remains to be done

  • Validate the problem with user research or trustworthy secondary research.
  • Sketch the current and proposed journey.
  • Define event properties and data-quality checks.
  • Confirm baseline metrics and experiment eligibility before proposing sample size.

Making surplus-food discovery more dependable

Domain: Climate & Sustainability

Project type: Independent product case study

Status: Independent concept; no provider partnerships, inventory data, or impact claims are implied.

1. Problem framing

People interested in low-cost surplus food may hesitate if offer details, pickup windows, or item availability are uncertain.

2. Target users

Local consumers seeking affordable food and participating food providers with surplus inventory.

3. Hypotheses to validate

  • Clearer pickup windows and availability status may reduce failed collections.
  • A reminder chosen by the user may improve collection completion.

4. Proposed product direction

  • Display quantity, collection window, price, and key food information before reservation.
  • Provide clear cancellation and no-show policies.
  • Let users opt into a single collection reminder.

5. Measurement plan

  • Primary candidate: reservation-to-collection completion rate.
  • Secondary: offer view-to-reservation conversion, repeat reservations, surplus units collected.
  • Guardrails: no-show rate, provider burden, food-safety complaints, reminder opt-outs.

6. Experiment approach

Pilot with a small provider group and compare collection completion before and after clearer offer details, accounting for provider and time-window differences.

7. Risks and guardrails

Never imply food safety beyond provider-supplied information. Ensure storage, allergen, expiry, and handling details are presented accurately.

8. What remains to be done

  • Validate the problem with user research or trustworthy secondary research.
  • Sketch the current and proposed journey.
  • Define event properties and data-quality checks.
  • Confirm baseline metrics and experiment eligibility before proposing sample size.
  • Record findings honestly; do not invent results.
